    A Life of Global Experience

    John’s career has crossed hospitality, international sports, travel, early internet technology, Y2K, the sharing economy, artificial intelligence and robotics.     Heritage & Global Innovation

    He grew up in Austin, Texas, in a family where entrepreneurship was part of daily life. His father died when John was young, and his mother operated restaurants and a travel agency. John and his siblings helped in the family businesses, giving him an early education in service, establishing a hard work ethic, responsibility, customer relationships and the realities of running a company.

    His mother was Greek, born and raised in Alexandria, Egypt, once one of the world’s great crossroads of knowledge, science, commerce and culture. His family’s heritage also reaches back to the island of Lemnos in the northern Aegean, where he spent summers as a child. FloLabs plans to establish its future headquarters, campus and AI and robotics laboratory there, with systems that will also support remote participation.

    John also lived for many years in Sofia, Bulgaria, where he built and managed international technology teams. That experience reinforced a conviction that now sits at the center of FloLabs: exceptional talent is not confined to the usual technology capitals.

    Each place contributed something different to how John thinks and works.

    John’s connection to education is also personal. According to his family history, his great grandfather was a philanthropist who helped fund a school in the village of Sardes on the island of Lemnos and another in Alexandria. John is carrying that commitment forward through a learning community designed for AI, remote collaboration and continuous technological change.

    Why the island of Lemnos matters

    People have lived on the island of Lemnos for thousands of years. Excavations near Hephaistia at Ouriakos found evidence of a hunting camp from the eleventh millennium BC, around 10,000 BC. Hephaistia became one of the island’s two principal cities and an important center for the worship of Hephaestus.

    Ancient Greek tradition places Hephaestus and his workshop on the island of Lemnos at volcanic Mount Mosychlos. He was the god of fire, blacksmiths, metalworking, craftsmanship, invention and innovation. Ancient accounts also credit him with creating Talos, a bronze humanoid robot, and Pandora, whom John views as an early description of an intelligent artificial being. The story of Pandora is associated with what is commonly called Pandora's Box.

    John believes these accounts may be rooted in real events and may preserve memories of advanced craftsmanship and technology that later generations described in the language available to them. He does not see them simply as made up stories.

    FloLabs plans to establish its future headquarters, campus and AI and robotics laboratory on the island of Lemnos. The campus and its remote systems will allow people from around the world to study, collaborate and build together. This work is part of Hephaestus.International and connects the island’s long history of craftsmanship and invention with the technologies shaping the future.

    Learning entrepreneurship by doing it

    John studied hotel and restaurant management at the University of Houston, but his most formative education came from building businesses.

    His first ventures involved practical work. He helped start an import and export business moving used clothing to Honduras and mahogany furniture to the United States. He cofounded a landscape maintenance company, operated crews and trucks, and later opened a travel agency near the University of Texas campus in Austin.

    Those experiences taught him lessons that rarely fit neatly into a textbook: how to earn trust, sell a service, manage cash flow, solve operational problems, recover from mistakes and keep moving when the original plan does not work.

    They also created unexpected opportunities.

    John and his brother first met the president of a women’s professional basketball team in Milan, Italy, when he came into their travel agency to buy an airline ticket. That chance meeting led them to help recruit a University of Texas player for the team. They later founded Agex International, a sports agency focused on representing American women’s basketball players in Europe and other international leagues. At the time, the United States did not yet have an established women’s professional league. Their clients played in Italy, Spain, France, Turkey, Japan and more than 20 other countries.

    John and his brother later invested proceeds from the basketball business in the Austin Soccadillos, a semiprofessional soccer franchise that John helped operate for two seasons. Ahead of the 1994 FIFA World Cup, he helped bring the Bulgarian national team to Austin for its training camp before the tournament and represented the delegation during its stay.

    These experiences taught John how to connect talented people with international opportunities and turn unexpected meetings into practical ventures.

    Entering internet travel before online booking was normal

    In the mid 1990s, while operating his travel agency, John began working with technologist and particle physicist Linas Vepstas on an early prototype that allowed travelers to search for and book flights through the internet.

    At the time, persuading people to enter a credit card number online was itself a frontier problem. Much of the infrastructure now taken for granted, including mature APIs, cloud platforms, simple payment integrations and modern web development frameworks, did not yet exist.

    John recognized that online travel would become a major market, but the company still needed capital, technical talent and time to rebuild its systems as the market changed.

    After more than a year of approaching potential investors, John secured backing from Admiral Bobby R. Inman, the former director of the U.S. National Security Agency and former deputy director of the CIA, who had gone on to become an investor and technology leader in Austin. Inman would become a long term adviser to John across multiple ventures.

    John established a development operation in Sofia, Bulgaria, building an international technology organization around internet travel and enterprise software. An early company and its Sofia team were acquired by the German travel technology company iFAO, which was later acquired by Amadeus, while John retained the travel booking code and continued building.

    He later launched TravelStoreMaker with investment from Worldspan, a global distribution system for travel, and additional backing from Admiral Inman. The platform provided private label internet booking technology, fulfillment and customer support to travel businesses in multiple countries. Thousands of small and midsized websites used the technology, including travel agencies and airlines. In today’s language, John describes the model as an early “Shopify for travel.”

    After the September 11 attacks disrupted global travel and Worldspan stopped making additional investments, John and his team had to support the business from its own revenue. They adapted the model and ultimately served travel businesses in more than 20 countries through sites available in more than 20 languages. The experience taught John that a capable team can survive disruption by conserving resources, changing direction and continuing to build.

    At its peak, the Sofia operation grew to more than 130 team members.

    The architecture behind TravelStoreMaker also created a direct technical connection to FloBrain. John’s teams integrated global distribution systems, more than 50 low cost carriers, hotels, rental cars and travel package services through a middleware platform that allowed clients to choose APIs and build their own interfaces. FloBrain applies a related orchestration mindset to AI models, data, tools and interfaces. The technologies are different, but the problem solving pattern is familiar: connect fragmented systems and make them useful through one experience.

    During this period, John also served for two years as a research analyst for Phocuswright, studying online travel trends in Central and Eastern Europe. He organized an online travel summit and, the following year, an online travel and space summit featuring astronaut Story Musgrave as keynote speaker. Musgrave flew six missions across all five Space Shuttle orbiters, and John witnessed three of his launches, two at night and one during the day, as well as a shuttle landing at Edwards Air Force Base. Those experiences formed an early bridge among travel, technology, research and space, connections that later reappeared in FloLabs’ space, robotics and citizen science initiatives.

    The travel technology years showed John how quickly new infrastructure, customer needs, investor priorities and outside events can change a business.

    Setbacks, reinvention and the value of changing direction

    John does not present entrepreneurship as a sequence of victories. He speaks openly about layoffs, failed fundraising efforts, difficult pivots and the emotional weight of watching a business contract.

    Around 2011, as major customers changed direction and the travel technology market evolved, he had to reduce the size of the Sofia team. Letting go of colleagues he had worked with for years was one of the most difficult periods of his career.

    In 2012, during that period of uncertainty, John joined 11 Bulgarian cyclists on a 55 day journey of more than 3,000 kilometers from Sofia to London. The group crossed 13 countries and arrived shortly before the London Summer Olympics.

    Along the way, the riders used Couchsurfing and the then emerging Airbnb platform. The experience gave John a firsthand view of the sharing economy and inspired his next venture.

    After returning to Austin, he founded sharingXchange, initially conceived as a metasearch platform for sharing economy accommodation and boat rental marketplaces. The company later evolved into a property listing and management service that helped owners distribute and support their properties across multiple booking channels. It grew to approximately 600 properties, primarily in Europe.

    Then the COVID pandemic stopped international travel and devastated the business.

    John adapted again. A houseboat he owned on Lake Travis became the prototype for a water based hospitality concept combining houseboat stays, events and glamping. That experience led to a broader resort concept, but after nearly two years of partnership and funding discussions, the project did not secure the support required to move forward.

    John’s work also extended into nonprofit and social impact initiatives. He cofounded Face to Face Bulgaria with a friend to combat sex trafficking and forced prostitution. In 2022, he cofounded GR4UA with members of his family to help Ukrainians living in Greece find employment after the war began. These efforts reflect a commitment to practical help that predates FloLabs and now informs its wider mission.

    The experience helped John decide where to focus next. He asked which field could create the greatest long term impact. His answer was artificial intelligence.

    Becoming a learner again

    John entered AI with decades of experience in technology and entrepreneurship, but without pretending to be an AI expert.

    He approached the transition as an intensive independent apprenticeship. For months, he spent 12 hours a day studying artificial intelligence, deep learning, emerging tools, recorded conferences, research, online courses and technical meetups. He attended AI communities in Austin and remotely followed events in San Francisco and elsewhere.

    As John studied AI, he reconnected with Linas Vepstas, the particle physicist and technology cofounder who had helped him build the early internet travel systems. Vepstas later served as a lead developer for OpenCog, an open source application framework for artificial general intelligence, or AGI, and as head of research for the AI behind Hanson Robotics’ Sophia. Their conversations helped John see how his experience connecting software and operating systems could apply to robotics.

    John encourages people not to let a previous degree, title or industry become a box. Experience does not make someone less capable of starting again; it creates a larger foundation from which to connect new ideas. He compares that accumulation to an expanding AI dataset: the richer the foundation, the easier it can become to learn and make new connections. His own transition from travel technology into AI, robotics, neuroscience, physics and biology is the evidence behind that belief.

    That experience exposed a gap that would later become central to FloLabs.

    The internet contains an extraordinary amount of knowledge. A motivated learner can access university lectures, research papers, certification programs, technical tutorials, open source software and expert discussions from almost anywhere. What remains difficult to obtain is the environment around the information: meaningful projects, capable collaborators, structured accountability, feedback, context and the chance to apply knowledge to something real.

    John did not need another collection of disconnected courses. He needed a reason to connect the dots.

    That insight became both a product philosophy and an educational philosophy.

    From CAIPO to FloBrain, embodied AI and robotics

    John’s first concept in this new chapter was CAIPO, originally derived from “Chief AI and Productivity Officer.” He envisioned an AI layer that could help organizations connect fragmented information, understand meetings and conversations, make knowledge usable and help people become more productive.

    The idea expanded into an AI wearable with a microphone, speaker, camera and sensors, an interface intended to provide context aware assistance in the physical world. John then returned to his roots in travel, imagining a conversational AI travel planner and travel companion that could understand preferences, build itineraries and eventually power a robotic hotel concierge.

    Those ideas led to FloBrain, a multimodal AI and contextual intelligence platform that brings together language models, voice, vision, sensor data, memory, tools and specialized AI buddies. FloLabs is developing it as a shared intelligence layer for software, wearables and robots.

    Cosmos Intelligence is one example. FloLabs is developing this conversational Space Buddy to combine NASA and other open data with artificial intelligence so people can explore the universe and ask questions naturally. John wants it to make the knowledge, discovery and wonder of space available to people everywhere, including those who may never travel there physically.

    Why John created FloLabs

    FloLabs began with a practical startup problem: ambitious AI and robotics projects required a team.

    John initially considered a conventional internship model. But an earlier experience recruiting interns at a university career event had shown him the limitations of unstructured internships. Many people joined enthusiastically, few continued, and some later listed months of experience without having completed meaningful work.

    He wanted to build something more serious for the participant and for the organization.

    FloLabs started with a small group of approximately 10 interns and grew into a global community spanning more than 20 countries. What began as a way to form project teams now combines:

    Experiential learning, where participants learn through relevant work.

    Structured masterclasses, aligned with the tools and knowledge a team actually uses.

    Applied research, connecting emerging ideas with practical experiments.

    A startup and venture studio, where ideas move from research and design toward development, testing and launch.

    International collaboration, bringing together people across disciplines, cultures and experience levels.

    Longer term pathways, including apprenticeship, fellowship, scholar, Masters, PhD and entrepreneurship tracks for participants who want to continue.

    John’s approach to learning and leadership

    Learn only what you can connect to action +

    FloLabs masterclasses are aligned with the work of each team. A participant learning AWS, Figma, data engineering, multimodal perception, automation or AI assisted development should have an opportunity to apply that knowledge to a relevant task.

    The goal is to build evidence of ability rather than collect certificates.

    Work with a buddy +

    FloLabs uses a buddy system because people generally learn faster and produce stronger work when they plan, build and review together. Participants break projects into tasks, agree on responsibilities, document the process and bring results back to the wider team.

    The buddy system builds technical skill while improving communication, reliability, project planning, peer learning and accountability.

    Develop integrity and reliability alongside technical ability +

    John believes education should develop character and professional habits as deliberately as it develops technical ability. FloLabs therefore places a strong emphasis on hard work, integrity and reliability, qualities that build trust and remain valuable in every industry, role and stage of a career.

    At FloLabs, integrity means being honest about what you know and do not know, giving others appropriate credit, representing your work accurately, acknowledging mistakes and allowing evidence to guide conclusions. In research, it also means stating uncertainty clearly and never exaggerating a result or making a claim before the evidence supports it.

    Reliability means showing up prepared, honoring commitments, communicating early when something is delayed or blocked, supporting teammates and completing agreed work to an appropriate standard. Intelligence and technical skill may create an opportunity, but integrity and reliability determine whether other people will trust someone with greater responsibility.

    Participants practice these values through documented work, transparent collaboration, the buddy system, peer accountability and consistent follow through. John tells participants to focus on hard work, integrity and reliability because all else will follow.

    See how a startup really works +

    John wants participants to see how a startup works beyond their own department. When appropriate, they may join conversations with advisers, partners, prospective customers and domain experts. They see how ideas are evaluated, why priorities change and what happens after a product launches.

    Build in public and connect the dots +

    FloLabs works in the open whenever it can. Its approach combines open source software, open science and open learning. Participants see how ideas are researched, challenged, built, tested and improved. The Connecting the Dots podcast shares selected team discussions so others can follow the work.

    Remain a learner at every level +

    Calling himself the founding intern is John’s reminder that leadership does not place someone above the learning process. The technologies FloLabs works with change too quickly for static expertise. Curiosity, humility, discernment and the ability to learn continuously are strategic advantages.

    Pursue work with meaning and purpose +

    John focuses on AI and robotics because he believes they can expand human capability, improve health, increase access, create opportunity and solve useful problems.

    The FloLabs mission

    FloLabs’ mission is:

    We aspire to inspire, help as many people as possible, live long and prosper.

    The Vulcan salute appears in the FloLabs identity because John believes technology and entrepreneurship should improve life.

    John defines prosperity broadly to include health, knowledge, meaningful work, community, personal agency and the ability to contribute something valuable.

    That is why FloLabs connects education with research and venture creation. Learning should lead somewhere. Research should inform action. Technology should serve people. Entrepreneurship should create pathways for others, not only rewards for founders.

    A personal invitation from John

    I have spent my career moving between industries, countries and technology cycles. I have built companies, raised investment, managed international teams, watched ventures grow and experienced the pain of having to start again.

    The most important lesson I have learned is that no one ever reaches a point where learning is finished.

    When I entered artificial intelligence, I had decades of experience in technology, but I was a beginner again. I spent months studying, joining communities and trying to connect what I was learning to real problems. That experience helped me understand what is missing from much of education today. Knowledge is available everywhere, but meaningful opportunities to apply it are not.

    FloLabs is our attempt to change that.

    We are building a global community where serious learners can work with one another on ideas at the frontier of AI, robotics, research and entrepreneurship. Some participants will use the experience to strengthen a university education. Some will change careers. Some will become researchers, engineers or founders. Others will join because they want to contribute to projects that give them meaning and purpose.

    The program requires real work and does not guarantee a job. It offers a place to develop practical ability and join a community building useful technology.

    If you are relentlessly curious, willing to contribute and ready to learn by doing, I invite you to explore FloLabs.
